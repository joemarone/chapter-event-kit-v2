# Mastery Rubric

This is how reviewers grade the **mastery** field of a Spark submission. The builder shows it inline while you write so you can self-check before submitting.

Mastery is the most-rewritten field in the entire submission. Read this once and you'll skip a revision round.

> Refresher from [anatomy.md](anatomy.md#the-mastery-component): for a Spark, mastery is **a single threshold** — one sentence describing what the kid can do, on demand, cleanly, that they couldn't do before.

---

## The five tests

A passing mastery statement clears all five. Reviewers will mark each one ✓ or ✗. Any ✗ sends the submission back with that specific reason.

### 1. Watchable
You could film the kid attempting the threshold and an outside observer (someone who didn't run the Spark) could tell you whether they passed or failed.

| ✓ Pass | ✗ Bounce |
|---|---|
| "Solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube in under 3 minutes." | "Understand how the Rubik's cube works." |
| "Tie a bowline knot in under 30 seconds." | "Appreciate the importance of knots." |
| "Recite the U.S. presidents in order without a prompt." | "Develop an interest in American history." |

**The kill words:** *understand, appreciate, learn about, explore, become familiar with, gain exposure to, develop a love of.* If your mastery statement contains any of these, rewrite it.

### 2. Binary
The result is yes or no. There is no "kind of," no "made progress toward," no partial credit.

| ✓ Pass | ✗ Bounce |
|---|---|
| "Juggle 3 balls for 10 consecutive throws." | "Get better at juggling." |
| "Light a single match on the first strike, outdoors." | "Improve at fire-starting." |

**The fix:** if your statement has a "kind of" hidden in it, find the threshold that turns it binary. *"Got better at juggling"* becomes *"10 consecutive throws."* The number is doing real work.

### 3. Sharp bar
The threshold is specific enough that two different facilitators grading the same kid would reach the same verdict. No interpretive room.

| ✓ Pass | ✗ Bounce |
|---|---|
| "Name all 50 U.S. states on a blank map in under 90 seconds." | "Name a lot of states." |
| "Solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube in under 3 minutes." | "Solve a Rubik's cube reasonably fast." |
| "Sing a verse of the song from memory, on key, with no prompts." | "Sing the song well." |

**The fix:** add a number, a time, a count, or a precise condition. If two facilitators could disagree about whether the kid passed, the bar isn't sharp yet.

### 4. Earned
The threshold is high enough that a typical kid in the target age range cannot pass it on day one *even with* the Spark's coaching unless they take the practice seriously. The Spark has to teach a real, kept skill — not host a party trick.

This is the strictest test. The library is positioned next to the existing curated workshops, so every Spark has to clear a real skill bar.

| ✓ Pass | ✗ Bounce |
|---|---|
| "Solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube in under 3 minutes." | "Tie a basic overhand knot." |
| "Juggle 3 balls for 10 consecutive throws." | "Balance a spoon on your nose for 5 seconds." |
| "Recite all 50 U.S. states from a blank map in under 90 seconds." | "Name 5 states." |
| "Sight-read and play a short piano piece you've never seen." | "Press a few keys on a piano." |

**The kill list — none of these will pass Earned:**
- Party tricks (spoon-on-nose, basic card tricks, simple knots)
- Anything you can google in the morning and demo five minutes later
- Silly bits, gag activities, "dumb magic"
- Crafts whose only outcome is the craft itself, with no demonstrable skill the kid keeps

**The fix:** if you handed your Spark idea to a stranger's kid with five minutes of YouTube and no facilitator, would they pass the threshold? If yes, the bar is too low — raise it or pick a different Spark.

### 5. Single
One threshold. Not three. Compound mastery statements ("can do A *and* B *and* C") are really three separate Sparks pretending to be one.

| ✓ Pass | ✗ Bounce |
|---|---|
| "Solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube in under 3 minutes." | "Solve a Rubik's cube, explain the algorithm, and teach it to a friend." |
| "Recite all 50 U.S. states from a blank map." | "Recite the states, name the capitals, and identify each on a map." |

**The fix:** pick the *one* test that, if the kid passes it, you'd say with a straight face that they got the win. Drop the rest. (They can become future Sparks if they're good.)

## Worked example: a real revision round

This is the kind of revision a typical first submission goes through. Same Spark idea, three drafts.

**Draft 1 (rejected — fails Watchable, Binary, Sharp bar):**
> Kids will learn to juggle and develop better hand-eye coordination, gaining confidence in their physical abilities.

Reviewer: "Learn" and "develop" aren't watchable. No threshold at all. What does the kid actually have to *do*?

**Draft 2 (sent back — fails Sharp bar, Earned):**
> Juggle 3 balls.

Reviewer: Better, but "juggle 3 balls" could mean one toss-and-catch or it could mean a real run. How long? Also — can they already do one toss? Tighten the bar so it's a real test.

**Draft 3 (approved):**
> Juggle 3 balls for at least 10 consecutive throws without dropping, on demand, in front of the group.

Reviewer: ✓ Watchable (you can count throws). ✓ Binary (10 or not). ✓ Sharp (specific number, specific condition). ✓ Earned (typical 9-year-old can't do this on day one). ✓ Single (one test).

---

## Reviewer decision

After grading the five tests:

- **5 ✓** → Approve the mastery field. Move on to grading the other fields.
- **3–4 ✓** → **Request changes**, citing the specific failed tests. The submitter usually fixes these in one pass.
- **0–2 ✓** → Likely a deeper issue with the Spark itself, not just the mastery wording. Reviewer notes should suggest the submitter re-read [anatomy.md](anatomy.md) and reconsider whether the Spark idea is sharp enough yet.

---

## A note for submitters

If you're staring at your mastery field and it doesn't pass these tests, that's almost always a signal that the *Spark itself* isn't sharp yet — not just the wording. The mastery field is the easiest place to feel that vagueness, because it's where you have to commit to what success actually looks like.

When that happens: don't keep wordsmithing. Step back, ask "what's the one thing the kid can demonstrably do at the end?", and rebuild the whole submission around that answer.
