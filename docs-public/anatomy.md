# Anatomy of a Spark

This doc is the source of truth for what makes a good Spark. The builder reads from it. Reviewers grade against it. Parents use it as a writing guide.

If you're submitting a Spark to the chapter library for the first time: read this once before you start the builder. It'll save you a revision round.

---

## What a Spark is

A Spark is a tightly contained, low-prep, "feels-impossible-but-isn't" experience that a parent can host in their living room, backyard, or a corner of a coffee shop with five minutes of setup.

The whole point is repeatability. A Spark you build well, *anyone* in the chapter network can run.

| Trait | Spark |
|---|---|
| **Length** | 10–30 minutes |
| **Prep** | Show up and go. Maybe one prop or a small bag of supplies. |
| **Arc** | One concrete trick, technique, or moment — repeated until it clicks |
| **Group size** | Small huddle, or one-at-a-time mentoring |
| **Mastery** | One single threshold — they can do the thing, on demand, cleanly |
| **What kids leave with** | A trick they own, that they can show off tonight |

If your idea takes more than 30 minutes, needs stations, or needs a real run-of-show, it's not a Spark — and the chapter kit isn't the right home for it right now.

> **Rule of thumb:** if a parent could host it in their living room on a Tuesday with five minutes' notice, it's a Spark. If it can't, simplify until it is — or set it aside.

## The shared anatomy

Every Spark in the library has the same skeleton. Get these right and the rest follows.

### 1. Name
The handle the kit, the post, the calendar invite, and the kids all use.

- 1–4 words. Concrete.
- Names a thing or names a vibe — pick one.
- No colons, no taglines, no "Spark" in the name itself.
- It should be googleable as a unique phrase. If it's not, it's too generic.

### 2. One-liner
The single sentence that runs under the name everywhere. Has to do three things:

- Say what the kid actually does (verb-led).
- Hint at *why it's worth their 20 minutes*.
- Stay under ~25 words.

Good shape: *"Solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube from any starting position using a memorable seven-step method."*
Bad: *"A fun and exciting activity where kids will learn many valuable skills."* — vague, no verb, no specific outcome.

### 3. How the kids participate
One paragraph (3–6 sentences) describing what the kids are *physically and socially doing* during the Spark. Not the lesson plan. Not the learning objectives. The actual lived experience.

- Are they sitting? Moving? Watching first? Trying alongside? Going one at a time?
- What do their hands do?
- Where's the moment the trick clicks?

If a parent reading this can't picture themselves walking into the room and instantly knowing what's happening, rewrite it.

### 4. Materials
Two lists, kept separate:

- **Parents bring** — what each kid (or family) is responsible for. *Empty is allowed and great* — the kit will say "Nothing to bring."
- **Facilitator provides** — what the host supplies for the whole group.

Rules:
- One item per line.
- Concrete and shoppable. "Marbles" not "small round things." "Felt squares (assorted colors)" not "fabric."
- A Spark with more than ~5 items on the facilitator side is probably not a Spark anymore. Trim or split.

### 5. Cost
What it costs each attending family. Three options, in order of preference:

- **Free** (best — most chapters can absorb supply costs)
- **A flat number** ("$5 per attendee")
- **A range** only if the format genuinely varies ("$5–$15 depending on what you bring")

If you're charging more than $15/kid, the submission needs a justification — reviewers will push back on anything higher without one. Sparks are designed to be cheap; that's part of why they're repeatable.

## The mastery component

This is the hardest field to write and the one that most often gets sent back for revision. It's also the field that makes the difference between "we did an activity" and "the kid walked away changed."

Mastery is **the answer to: what can this kid now do, on demand, that they couldn't before?**

For a Spark, mastery is a **single threshold.** One sentence. A bar. They can do it cleanly, on demand, in front of someone, or they can't.

Examples of well-shaped mastery thresholds:

- "Can solve a 3×3 Rubik's cube in under 3 minutes without help."
- "Can light a fire from a single match outdoors on the first try."
- "Can name every U.S. state when shown a blank map, in under 90 seconds."
- "Can juggle three balls for at least 10 consecutive throws."

The test is binary: can they do it, yes or no? If your test has a "kind of," sharpen it until it doesn't.

### Common failure modes (will get sent back)
- **Vague verbs.** "Understand," "learn about," "appreciate," "explore." Replace with watchable verbs.
- **Effort instead of outcome.** "Tries hard at..." is not mastery. We're grading what came out, not what went in.
- **Too easy.** If every kid passes on the first attempt with no coaching, the bar is too low. Sparks should *feel* impossible at the start.
- **Compound tests.** "Can do A and B and C" is three Sparks. Pick one.

## Hard · Challenging · Impossible

Every Spark sits at one of three difficulty registers. The submitter picks one. The reviewer agrees or pushes back.

All three rungs are high. Hard is not the easy tier — it's the entry point of a high-skill ladder. Every Spark in the library, at every register, has to clear a real skill threshold. Each rung asks more of the kid and more of the facilitator than the one below it.

### What does **not** qualify — at any tier
Before you pick a tier, make sure your Spark belongs in the library at all. The library is *not* a place for:

- Party tricks (balancing a spoon on your nose, tying a basic knot, simple card tricks).
- Anything a kid could google in the morning and demo five minutes later, smiling, saying "this is fun."
- Silly bits, gag activities, or "dumb magic" with no real skill underneath.
- Crafts whose only outcome is the craft itself (no demonstrable skill the kid keeps).
- Things the average kid in the age range could already do on day one without coaching.

If your idea fits any of those, it's not a Spark — it's an activity. Set it aside.

### Hard
A real, kept skill that takes meaningful practice to acquire, but the path is well-defined. With clear instruction and the time box, most kids walk out *able to do the thing*. They keep it.

- Hard is high. Pick this when the threshold is something a kid could reasonably show off to a grandparent and the grandparent would be impressed.
- Example shape: read sheet music well enough to play a short piece you've never seen; throw an accurate spiral over 15+ yards; whistle loud enough with two fingers to stop a room; recite a meaningful set from memory (full presidents, all 50 states, etc.).

### Challenging
There is a real bar and not every kid will clear it on the first try. Some kids need a second attempt, some get coached through, and that struggle is *the point*.

- A Challenging Spark without visible struggle is mislabeled.
- These work best when the facilitator can actively coach. Tight groups, more attempts.
- Example shape: juggle 3 balls for 10 consecutive throws; tie a real working knot under time pressure with a specific application; sight-read a piece of music you haven't seen before.

### Impossible
At first glance, the kid looks at the goal and tells you they can't do it. Then, by the end of the session, they can. The arc *is* the experience.

- The most memorable Sparks live here. They also take the most facilitator skill.
- The submission has to credibly explain *how* the impossible becomes possible — what the unlock is. If a reviewer can't see the unlock, it gets sent back.
- Example shape: solve a Rubik's cube; recite all 50 states from a blank map; perform a real instrument piece for an audience.

### Picking
- Honest self-grade: would the average 9-year-old in your chapter say "I can't do that" when they hear the goal? If yes → Impossible. If they'd say "I think I can try" → Challenging. If they'd say "I'll need to learn this" → Hard.
- If they'd say *"sure, easy"* — it doesn't belong in the library at all.
- Don't reach for Impossible to make a Spark sound bigger than it is. A well-run Hard Spark beats a poorly-staged Impossible one every time.

## Quick self-check before you submit

Read your draft. If you can't answer all of these in one breath, keep editing:

1. In one sentence: what does the kid *do*?
2. In one sentence: what can they do at the end that they couldn't at the start?
3. Could a parent who's never run this read your guide and run it next Saturday with five minutes' setup?
4. Is the mastery test binary — yes/no, on demand?
5. Did you pick Hard, Challenging, or Impossible — and would another parent agree?

If yes to all five, submit it.
